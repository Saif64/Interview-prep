# Practising Out Loud

The biggest gap between people who prepare a lot and people who pass: **solving problems
silently at a desk is not the skill being tested.** The interview is a spoken, observed,
time-boxed performance, and it needs to be practised as one.

If you do one thing beyond solving problems, do this.

---

## Why it matters more than it used to

First rounds increasingly involve being recorded, being scored on delivery, or working
alongside an AI assistant while an interviewer watches how you do it. All three reward people
who can *narrate their thinking*. None of them reward silent competence.

You cannot fake this on the day. It is a motor skill; it needs reps.

---

## The minimum viable practice

**Solve one problem a day out loud, alone, to a timer.** No audience needed.

1. Set 30 minutes.
2. Read the problem and **speak** your clarifying questions, as if someone were there.
3. Narrate continuously: the brute force, the complexity, the bottleneck, the pattern.
4. Code while explaining what you are writing.
5. Trace an example out loud. Name the edge cases out loud.

It feels ridiculous for about three days, and then it feels normal — which is exactly the
point, because it needs to feel normal on the day.

**Record yourself once a week** and watch it back. You will be unpleasantly surprised, and
that is the most useful feedback available for free. Look for: long silences, filler, jumping
to code, never stating complexity, never testing.

---

## With a person

Worth much more, because a human interrupts, hints, and pushes back — which is what a real
interviewer does.

**Free:**
- **Pramp / Exponent peer mocks** — you interview someone, they interview you
- **interviewing.io** — anonymous mocks, some free with engineers from large companies
- **A friend in the industry** — underrated; give them the problem and the rubric beforehand
- **Discord / Reddit study groups** — r/cscareerquestions and language-specific servers

**Paid:** interviewing.io, Exponent, Meetapro, and independent coaches. Worth it in the last
two weeks before a loop you care about, rarely worth it early.

**How many:** at least two full mocks before an on-site. The first one is always bad. That is
the entire reason to do the first one.

## Using a model as your interviewer

Not as good as a person, available at 2am. It works well for the *narration* half.

A prompt that actually produces useful practice:

> "You are a senior engineer interviewing me for a backend role. Give me one medium-difficulty
> problem. Do not give me the solution or hints unless I ask. After I explain my approach,
> push back on it and ask about complexity and edge cases. At the end, score me on problem
> solving, coding, verification, and communication, and tell me the single thing that would
> most improve my performance."

Then **type your reasoning as you would say it**. The value is in articulating, not in the
problem.

It is also the best available practice for the AI-allowed round format — see
[ai-era-interviews.md](ai-era-interviews.md) — because the thing being graded there is
exactly this: how you direct, question, and verify a model in front of someone.

## What to actually work on

After each mock, pick **one** thing. Not five.

Common ones, roughly in order of how much they cost people:

| Problem | Fix |
| :--- | :--- |
| Long silences | Narrate the dead ends too: "a heap doesn't help here because…" |
| Coding immediately | Force yourself to state the brute force and its complexity first |
| Never testing | Build the habit: trace one example before you say you're done |
| Rambling | Signpost: "three steps — first…, then…, finally…" |
| Freezing when stuck | Practise the escape ladder in [coding-interview-playbook.md](coding-interview-playbook.md) |
| Not asking questions | Write three standard clarifiers on a sticky note |

## Behavioural mocks

People rehearse coding and wing the behavioural round, then lose on it.

Say your six stories out loud, to a timer, until each lands in two to three minutes without
notes. Record one. Watch for: too much setup, "we" instead of "I", no numbers, no outcome.

See [behavioral.md](behavioral.md) for what the six stories should be.
