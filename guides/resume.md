# The Resume

The highest-leverage document in your job search, because it gates every other stage — and
the one most engineers spend the least time on.

---

## The rules that matter

**One page**, unless you have 10+ years and genuinely cannot cut further. Nobody reads page
two.

**No photo, no age, no marital status, no "References available on request".**

**Plain, single-column, standard fonts.** Applicant tracking systems mangle multi-column
layouts, tables, headers, and text inside graphics. If a human never sees your resume because
the parser choked, the design was not worth it.

**PDF**, named `Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf`.

**Order:** Contact → (optional one-line summary) → Experience → Projects → Skills →
Education. Education goes last unless you graduated within the last year.

---

## Bullets are the whole game

The difference between a resume that gets calls and one that does not is almost entirely in
the bullets.

**The formula:** *Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z].*

> **Weak:** "Worked on the payments team to improve the checkout system."
>
> **Strong:** "Cut checkout p99 latency from 800ms to 120ms by replacing a synchronous
> fraud check with a queued one, removing ~40% of timeout-driven cart abandonment."

The second one is specific, measurable, and tells the reader what you actually decided.

**Every bullet should answer "so what?"** If it describes a task rather than an outcome,
rewrite it.

| Instead of | Write |
| :--- | :--- |
| "Responsible for maintaining the API" | "Cut API error rate from 2.1% to 0.3% by adding retry-with-backoff and fixing a connection-pool leak" |
| "Used React and Node" | "Shipped the customer dashboard used by ~4,000 weekly users; reduced support tickets about order status by a third" |
| "Participated in code reviews" | "Introduced a review checklist that cut average PR turnaround from 3 days to under 1" |

**No numbers?** You have more than you think: users, requests, latency, error rates, build
times, team size, time saved, tickets closed, cost. Estimate honestly and say "approximately"
if you must. "Reduced build time from ~12 min to ~4 min" is fine.

---

## Start every bullet with a verb

Built, shipped, designed, migrated, cut, automated, led, debugged, scaled, instrumented,
rewrote, deprecated.

Not: "Helped with", "Was involved in", "Worked on", "Responsible for".

---

## Projects

Worth including when you are early-career, changing specialisation, or your day job is under
NDA. One or two, not six.

Each needs: what it does in one line, what you built it with, a **link that works**, and a
number if one exists (users, stars, downloads, requests served).

A project nobody can look at is nearly worthless on a resume. Link it.

---

## Skills

A plain list, grouped. No bar charts, no star ratings, no "9/10 in Kotlin" — those invite the
question "so what's the 10?" and answer nothing.

Only list what you would be comfortable being interviewed on. Interviewers pick from this
section.

> **Languages:** Kotlin, Java, JavaScript, Python
> **Mobile:** Android SDK, Jetpack Compose, Coroutines, Flutter
> **Backend:** Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, Redis
> **Tools:** Git, Docker, Gradle, GitHub Actions

---

## Tailoring, briefly

You do not need a fresh resume per application. You do need the top third to match the job.

Read the posting, take the words they use for the things you have actually done, and use those
words. If they say "distributed systems" and you wrote "microservices", change it. This is not
gaming the ATS; it is not making a keyword-matcher guess.

---

## Before you send it

- Every number is defensible if asked about in an interview
- No typos — read it backwards, then have someone else read it
- Links work, and go where you think they go
- Consistent tense: present for the current role, past for everything else
- No unexplained employment gaps longer than a few months (one line is enough)
- It is genuinely one page
- Someone who does not know your work can tell what you actually do

---

## The artefact test

Pick your three strongest bullets. For each, ask: **if an interviewer asked "tell me more
about that", do I have three minutes of real detail?**

If not, either you are overselling it, or you have not thought about it enough. Both are
fixable before someone asks — and one of them is much better to fix beforehand.
