# Negotiation

The highest hourly-rate conversation of your career, and the one engineers are worst at.
A fifteen-minute conversation routinely moves an offer by five figures, and it compounds:
every future raise, bonus, and equity refresh is a percentage of the number you accept now.

Nothing here requires being aggressive. It requires being prepared.

---

## Before the first call

**Know your numbers before anyone asks.** Once an offer is on the table you are negotiating
under pressure; decide these while you are calm:

- **Floor** — below this you walk. Be honest with yourself.
- **Target** — the researched, defensible number you are actually aiming for.
- **Walk-away terms** — non-salary things that are genuine deal-breakers (remote, on-call,
  team, visa support).

**Research the range.** levels.fyi, Glassdoor, the company's public pay bands where those
exist, and — most usefully — people you know at that level in that market. Anchor to the
market, never to your current salary.

---

## The one rule

**Do not name a number first.** Whoever anchors first loses ground, and the recruiter asks
early precisely because it works.

When asked what you are looking for:

> "I'd rather understand the scope and level first — I want to make sure we're talking about
> the same role. What range has the team budgeted for this position?"

If they insist, give a **researched range**, not your current salary, and make the bottom of
that range a number you would genuinely be happy with:

> "Based on what I've seen for this level in this market, I'd expect something in the range
> of X to Y, depending on the whole package."

In jurisdictions with pay-transparency laws the posted band is your anchor. Use it.

**Never lie about a current or competing offer.** It is checkable, it is occasionally
checked, and it ends the process instantly when it is caught.

---

## When the offer arrives

**Do not accept on the call.** Not even a great offer. Say thank you, sound genuinely
pleased, and buy time:

> "That's great news, thank you — I'm really excited about the team. Could you send the full
> details in writing? I'd like to look at the whole package properly and come back to you in
> a couple of days."

Any legitimate employer says yes. An "exploding offer" that must be answered in 24 hours is a
red flag about how the company treats people, and it is worth naming as one.

---

## The counter

One clear, specific, warm counter. Not a list of grievances.

> "I'm genuinely excited about this role and I'd like to make it work. Based on my research
> for this level, and given [specific relevant experience], I was hoping for base closer to
> X. Is there flexibility there?"

Then **stop talking.** The silence is uncomfortable and it is doing work. Do not negotiate
against yourself by filling it.

**Give a reason.** "Because I want more" is weak; "because the market rate for this level is
X and I bring Y" is a reason they can take to their approver. You are arming an ally, not
fighting an opponent — the recruiter usually has to justify the increase internally.

---

## What is negotiable beyond base salary

Base is often the most constrained part. These are frequently easier:

- **Signing bonus** — usually the most flexible single item
- **Equity** — larger grant, or faster vesting
- **Level** — the biggest long-term lever by far; a level bump is worth more than any bonus
- **Start date**
- **Remote or hybrid arrangement**
- **Relocation**
- **Vacation** beyond the standard allocation
- **Learning budget, equipment, conference budget**
- **An early review** — "a comp review at six months rather than twelve"

If base truly cannot move, ask which of these can. Something almost always can.

---

## Competing offers

The strongest leverage that exists, which is why batching applications matters.

Use it plainly and without threatening:

> "I have another offer at X. I'd rather join you — is there room to close the gap?"

Do not invent one. Do not play companies off against each other theatrically. State it once,
honestly, and let it work.

---

## What not to do

- Accept on the spot
- Name your current salary (in many places they may not even ask)
- Apologise for negotiating — it is a normal, expected part of hiring
- Negotiate over five separate emails; do it in one or two rounds
- Threaten to walk unless you actually will
- Keep pushing after a clear final answer — take the win and start well

---

## After you agree

**Get it in writing before you resign from anything.** The full package: base, bonus
structure, equity grant and vesting schedule, start date, level, and anything you negotiated
verbally. A verbal promise that is not in the letter does not exist once your contact changes
role.

Then stop negotiating and start the job well. The relationship matters more than the last
two thousand.
